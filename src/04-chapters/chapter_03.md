Chapter 3 — The Limit

Keji carried the hidden memory like a stone in her palm. The city above the hall moved by habit: footsteps, murmurs, the hiss of data being fed into public walls. The Curators' hall was cooler than the market, and the air there tasted faintly of paper and old breath.

Ari was at his desk. He did not look up when she came in. He sorted a stack of seals with deliberate fingers. When she laid the glitch on the wood, his hand paused over a stamp.

"You went deeper than you should have," he said without looking at her.

Keji did not answer. She stuck the coin into her pocket and felt its warm edge through the fabric. She held the glitch flat on the desk until it stopped humming.

He finally met her eyes. "Some doors are not for us. They close because what lies behind them cuts too deep."

She slid the child's looped J toward him. "This was in the wall. The curve matches Lio's hand."

Ari's mouth tightened. His fingers left the seals and drifted over the paper as if counting its weight. "This is a private ledger," he said. "Not for circulation."

"Then why is it in a public script?" Keji asked.

Ari's breath came out slow. "Because the public layer must remain stable. The hidden one can be messy."

He pushed back his chair. He stood, then hesitated. "There is a sequence. Authorization. A date. A signature. The document is old. It has been sealed for years. If you are going to touch it, do it quietly." He did not say he had once touched it himself.

He turned away and walked to a cabinet. The drawer opened with the sound of hinges that had been oiling themselves for decades. He took out a folder with a name she knew and a timestamp she did not. He did not let her see the pages, only the edge of the paper when he slid it back.

"Ira Nash signed the authorization," Ari said. "The rest is redacted."

The name caught under her ribs. The hidden space between the sound of his words and the texture of the paper felt colder.

She left the hall with the glitch back in her sleeve, the coin in her pocket, and the weight of the redacted pages at the back of her eyes. Outside, the market walls glowed. A street vendor shouted about a new excavation in the public archive. People moved with the easy certainty of those whose memories were arranged for them.

Sera met her at the edge of the Drift, where the stone had been worn by hands and names. She leaned against a wall that breathed slow. "You found something," she said.

Keji tapped the pocket with her thumb. "A seal. A child’s loop. A voice."

Sera's grin went flat for a moment. "Good. It's not enough to have a suspicion. It's something to touch."

They walked through a passage where the wall script was live. When Keji reached out to trace a line, the ink dimmed under her finger. The letters rearranged themselves into a pattern that was not quite a word.

"Don't let it shape you," Sera murmured. "Let it show you the shape." She pressed her hand beside Keji's. The letters held for them together, then flickered and vanished.

"The Limit is not a voice yet," Sera said. "It's the thing the city does when it decides you're asking for too much."

Keji watched a block of script on the wall. It had been smoothed into a bright veneer. She touched it. The surface was warm and pitted. Under her skin the wall seemed to pulse. OPEN flashed and then was gone.

They moved deeper into the Drift. Memory flared around them in small bursts: a child's laugh before it dissolved, a bureaucrat's apology that broke into a rhythmic click, a hallway that smelled of rain when there was no rain.

In an alley between two archive feeders, a living wall had been left unfinished. The surface there was rough and raw, as if whoever had been writing it had been interrupted. Keji could see a line of text half-formed beneath a layer of varnish.

She reached out. The letters felt like cold skin. She read what she could: ...AUTHORIZED... NO FURTHER... LIO... then the line dissolved into the wall's pulse.

The Limit was there. She could feel it as pressure along her spine, a refusal in the air.

Sera did not flinch. She held Keji's wrist. "You can follow where it wants to go," she said. "Or you can let it close the doors."

Keji's grip closed around the coin in her pocket. It was a thing from the same world the wall had been keeping away. She looked at Sera.

"I have to go where it is not willing to stay," Keji said.

Sera let go of her wrist and stepped aside. In the space she left, the Drift opened into a narrow stair. The walls there were not walls; they were layers of old recorders, each one humming with its own temperature. The stair dipped into a scent of wet stone and paper.

Keji took the first step.
