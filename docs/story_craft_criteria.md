# Craft Criteria: What Makes Stories Resonate

An independent compilation of features shared by stories that endure and
resonate, drawn from the Western craft tradition and modern story theory:
Aristotle's *Poetics* (mimesis, peripeteia, catharsis), Freytag's pyramid,
Campbell/Vogler's monomyth, McKee's *Story* (value charges, gap between
expectation and result), Swain/Bickham on scene structure (goal → conflict →
disaster), John Yorke's *Into the Woods* (five-act mirror structure, want vs.
need), K.M. Weiland and Brandon Sanderson on promise/progress/payoff and
magic-system economics, Le Guin's *Steering the Craft* and the
Hemingway/Chekhov tradition on prose (iceberg theory, Chekhov's gun),
Vonnegut on story shapes, and Pixar's story rules ("coincidences that create
trouble are useful; coincidences that resolve it are cheating").

Each criterion is written as a testable feature. Scoring anchors:

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Broken or absent; actively harms the work |
| 3–4 | Weak; gesture exists but doesn't function |
| 5 | Serviceable; does the job, nothing more |
| 6 | Solid; dependable professional work |
| 7 | Strong; clearly above average, memorable moments |
| 8 | Excellent; distinctive, would be praised in review |
| 9 | Rare; near-masterpiece execution of this trait |
| 10 | Canonical; defining example of the trait |

---

## 1. Foundation: Promise & Premise

1. **The hook** — The opening creates a question or imbalance quickly (first
   scene, ideally first page). Curiosity or unease precedes understanding.
2. **The promise** — Opening chapters make implicit contracts (genre, tone,
   scale of change: "mind-bending", "heartbreaking", "thriller") that the
   ending must honor. Breaking tone-promises breaks trust more than breaking
   plot-promises.
3. **The central dramatic question** — One question powers the whole book;
   it is concrete enough to answer and large enough to sustain the length.
   Sub-questions orbit it.

## 2. Structure

4. **Turning points** — Genuine reversals: moments where value changes and
   the protagonist's plan must change. Act breaks should be felt, not just
   countable. (Aristotle's peripeteia; McKee's gap.)
5. **Escalation through try-fail cycles** — Attempts cost something and each
   failure raises stakes or changes the game. Repeating the *same* attempt is
   not escalation; "yes, but / no, and" is.
6. **Midpoint shift** — Somewhere near center, the story's nature flips
   (reaction → action, personal → universal, question → answer-shaped).
7. **Scene-level drive** — Each scene has a goal, friction, and an outcome
   that changes information or value. Scenes that only confirm what we know
   are candidates for cutting.
8. **Pacing & rhythm variation** — Alternation of tension/release across
   chapters; quiet scenes earn their place by contrast, not default.

## 3. Character

9. **Want vs. need** — The protagonist pursues a conscious goal while
   unconsciously needing something else; the story's climax forces the
   distinction into a choice.
10. **Agency** — The protagonist causes events; luck and other characters'
    decisions may complicate, but the resolution belongs to her choices.
11. **Flaw with a price** — The protagonist's limitation demonstrably costs
    her something before the story rewards its correction.
12. **Relationships dramatized before they're leveraged** — Grief, love, and
    betrayal land in proportion to how much lived relationship the reader has
    witnessed. Absence can substitute for presence, but only if the absence
    is made vivid (artifacts, rituals, specific memories).
13. **Distinct voices** — Characters are identifiable from dialogue music
    alone (rhythm, diction, what they notice), not just content tags.

## 4. Antagonist & Opposition

14. **Mirror quality** — The antagonist embodies a twisted version of the
    protagonist's own desire; defeating/accepting them means confronting the self.
15. **Real pressure with consequences** — Opposition extracts costs: failures,
    losses, moral compromises. An antagonist whose "no" never costs anything
    is scenery.
16. **Presence and point-of-view pressure** — The antagonist is felt often
    enough to generate dread, and their logic is compelling enough to tempt
    the reader.

## 5. Mystery & Information Design

17. **Questions opened deliberately** — Every mystery raised is a promise;
    their number is managed so attention isn't diluted.
18. **Fair-play revelation** — Clues exist before reveals; rereading shows
    the truth was visible. Reveals recontextualize rather than contradict.
19. **Ambiguity as design, not evasion** — Productive ambiguity leaves the
    reader weighing two rich interpretations; thin ambiguity withholds so
    much that neither interpretation has substance. (The Limit-as-person vs.
    Limit-as-system needs evidence for both.)
20. **Reveal timing** — Information arrives at the latest moment that still
    allows consequence to play out; early reveals must change behavior.

## 6. Theme & Meaning

21. **Theme enacted, not stated** — The thematic argument lives in choices
    and outcomes (who wins, what it costs), not in dialogue debates or
    narrator commentary.
22. **Both sides have weight** — Opposing philosophies are given their best
    case and a real constituency; strawmen weaken the winner.
23. **Choice with moral weight** — The climax choice is genuinely costly;
    the reader feels what was traded away. A free victory is themeless.

## 7. Prose & Voice

24. **Clarity and specificity** — Concrete nouns, active verbs; every image
    could belong only to this story.
25. **Subtext** — Dialogue and description carry unspoken layers; text
    doesn't narrate the subtext.
26. **Rhythm and register control** — Sentence-length variance serving mood;
    a deliberate voice distinct from the author's defaults.
27. **Image economy** — Signature images recur with variation, not
    repetition; word-frequency tics (a noun appearing 100+ times) signal
    drift into mannerism.
28. **POV discipline** — Consistent perceptual filter; interiority rendered
    through sensation and action when withheld narration is the chosen style,
    at consistent depth.

## 8. Worldbuilding

29. **Integration** — World details are load-bearing for plot and character;
    nothing exists purely for tour-guide wonder.
30. **Consistent rules with felt costs** — The world's magic/system behaves
    predictably; its costs are paid on-page. (Sanderson's laws.)
31. **Defamiliarization with purpose** — The strange element reframes the
    familiar (memory editing → grief, archives → identity), rather than
    decorating familiar drama with novum.

## 9. Emotion & Resonance

32. **Stakes that escalate personally** — Abstract danger becomes concrete
    loss the reader can picture and dread.
33. **Emotional range** — The story modulates: warmth, humor, terror, anger —
    not one sustained note. Monotone emotion reads as affectlessness.
34. **Vulnerability on the page** — At least one moment where the
    protagonist is fully exposed; resonance requires witnessed fragility.
35. **Catharsis** — The ending releases the tension the story built
    (Aristotle); even ambiguous endings release *something* earned.

## 10. Ending

36. **Inevitable yet surprising** — In hindsight forced, in the moment fresh.
37. **Cost acknowledged** — The ending shows what was lost, not only what
    was settled.
38. **Transformation demonstrated** — Change shown in behavior, symbol, or
    echo — not asserted.
39. **Last-line landing** — Final sentence lands on image or beat that
    resonates forward beyond the page.

## 11. Genre Contract (self-referential test)

40. **Delivering the label's promise** — Whatever the work claims to be
    (its pitch, its genre tag), it delivers the core experience of that
    label at competitive quality.

# Part II — The Reading Experience

The text is not the story; the story is what happens in the reader. These
criteria measure the reading experience directly.

## 12. Reading Experience

41. **Tension curve shape** — Perceived tension, scored per scene, forms a
    deliberate curve: real peaks, earned valleys for release, no long flat
    stretches, chapter ends that pull forward.
42. **Open-loop management** — The reader's working questions are tracked:
    few enough to hold at once, none forgotten without a reminder beat, every
    one either answered or *deliberately* held by the end.
43. **Plant/payoff integrity** — Nothing planted goes unpaid; nothing paid
    off was never planted. Rereads reveal machinery, not accidents.
44. **Attachment engineering** — The reader is given reasons to care about
    each character *before* the story spends them: charm, vulnerability,
    competence, intimacy beats, scaled to how much loss the plot will demand.
45. **World-term onboarding** — Invented terms arrive with usable context
    within a chapter of first appearance; no term is used before it exists.
46. **Act-stakes legibility** — At every act break a cold reader could state
    what the protagonist wants and what failure costs.
47. **Expectation management** — Chapter turns mostly *shift* or *subvert*
    expectation rather than confirm it; subversions are seeded, not random.
48. **First-read frictionlessness** — A cold read produces no unproductive
    stumbles: no typos-as-obstacles, dangling referents, timeline snarls, or
    "wait, why didn't she just…" holes. Productive confusion (mystery) is
    exempt.
49. **Reread yield** — Specific early details gain meaning after the reveals;
    the second read is a different book.
50. **Reader judgment pressure** — The text asks the reader to take sides in
    a live disagreement and makes both sides tempting; the reader notices
    their own verdict forming.
