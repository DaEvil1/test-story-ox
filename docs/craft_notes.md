# The Craft Notes

What we learned about writing fiction by revising one novella through twelve
cycles of structured iteration, six experiments, three external reviews, and
enough analysis to develop opinions about opinions.

This is for a writer working alone, without AI tools, who wants to apply
what the process taught us. Some of this will be familiar from craft books.
Some of it won't, because we discovered it by watching a system make
mistakes efficiently enough that the mistakes became visible as patterns.

---

## Part I — What Actually Improves a Draft

### 1. Addition beats correction

Across twelve revision cycles, the changes that moved the needle were almost
always things we *added* — a lived memory, a physical cost, an ally's
independent motive, a detail that went nowhere. Fixing existing sentences
improved polish; adding missing elements improved the book.

**Practice:** After your second draft, stop editing sentences. Instead,
make two lists:
- What does my protagonist DO that I haven't shown?
- What has been taken FROM them that I haven't shown the loss of?

Then add those. Don't fix anything until you've added everything.

### 2. Costs must be personal and irreversible

"The world might end" is abstract. "She lost two fingers" is concrete.
But even better: "She can never go back to where she learned the truth."

The cost that lands hardest isn't pain or death — it's **permanent exclusion
from something the character wanted**. Access denied. A relationship that
can't return to what it was. Knowledge that changes how a familiar room
looks forever.

**Test:** Can your character return to the state they were in before the
climax? If yes, the stakes aren't high enough yet. Something must be
*finished*, not just difficult.

### 3. Allies need lives outside the protagonist's orbit

A supporting character who appears only when the protagonist needs support
is furniture with dialogue. Give each ally at least one scene where their
own need drives action, at least one disagreement with the protagonist that
isn't resolved within the same paragraph, and at least one moment where they
pay a cost for reasons that predate the protagonist's arrival.

**Test:** Remove your protagonist from the story. Do three supporting
characters still have problems worth reading about? If not, they're props.

### 4. Texture that goes nowhere is not wasted

A wooden button half-trodden into the floor of a chamber that matters to
the plot. A vendor's greeting that never recurs. A detail about a dead
woman's vowels. These go nowhere — and that is why they make the setting
feel inhabited rather than built.

If every detail connects to something else, the book reads as constructed.
Leave some threads loose. Leave some questions unanswered. Let the world
contain things that exist for their own sake.

**Practice:** In your final pass, identify three details you could cut
without losing anything. Then don't cut them. They're doing work you can't
measure.

## Part II — Emotional Craft

### 5. Emotional breaks must be involuntary

"She did not remember deciding to." — the ribbon winding tighter without
conscious choice. This reveals more than any described breakdown because it
shows the body processing what the mind won't authorize.

Compare: "She began to cry." — accurate but inert. The reader observes grief.
vs: "Her hands wound the ribbon around her fingers—once, twice, tighter than
good." — the reader *feels* grief displacing motor control.

**Practice:** Find your climax emotional beat. Replace any sentence that
names the emotion ("she felt devastated") with one that shows an action the
body performs without permission.

### 6. One word can replace five

"That is enough for now" → "Enough."

This only works when ten chapters have established the character's voice as
precise. Brevity as finality is earned by everything before it. But when it
lands, it lands harder than a paragraph.

**Practice:** Find your protagonist's last spoken line. Could it be shorter?
Could it be one word?

### 7. Silence is a beat

After a character says something important, the next thing that happens
should be silence or physical action — not another person's response. Give
the reader time to feel the weight before anyone else reacts to it.

"The lamp buzzed once and stopped."

## Part III — Structural Craft

### 8. Write the ending before you connect the middle

Write your first chapter and your last chapter before writing anything
between them. Know who your character is before and after. Don't plan the
middle — just hold the gap between those two states in your mind and start
writing toward it. The gap IS the story.

When the middle takes you somewhere unexpected: follow it. Your ending may
need to change. That's fine. Endings written before middles tend to be
strawmen anyway — you'll discover a better one once you know who your
character has become.

### 9. Follow the thread past its apparent importance

When you notice yourself thinking "checkmate" or "this feels like the wrong
game," don't solve the immediate problem. Pull the thread: what game WOULD
belong here? What would this society invent? What impossible position would
terrify someone?

Most threads contain nothing. Some contain the book. You can't tell which
without following them further than seems reasonable.

### 10. Physical warnings should escalate before physical consequences

If your character will eventually lose something bodily, plant smaller
versions earlier. A hum under the fingernails in chapter 2. A tremor in
chapter 4. A numbness that fades in chapter 6. By the time the door takes
two fingers in chapter 9, the reader understands: this place charges for
access, and the price increases with proximity.

Without escalation, the injury feels like a trap-door mechanism. With
escalation, it feels like an inevitable consequence of violating a boundary
the character was warned about — and chose to violate anyway.

## Part IV — Pattern Awareness

These are tics you cannot feel while drafting but can see when measured.
You don't need software — a careful reread with these specific patterns in
mind is sufficient.

### 11. Count your openers

Take your manuscript and note the first word of every sentence. If any
single word starts more than 15% of your sentences, you have drifted into
a pattern you can't feel. Canon prose rarely exceeds ~10% on any opener.

### 12. Count your body-part metonymy

"Her throat closed." "His chest tightened." "Her breath hitched." These are
valid individually. Collectively, above roughly 12 per 10,000 words, they
become a formula the reader recognizes without naming.

### 13. Listen for the negation-then-affirmation frame

"It was not X. It was Y." / "Not X. Y." / "She did not X. She wrote Y."
Useful sparingly. Habitual use creates a rhythm of false certainty. If more
than 2–3 appear per chapter, vary the construction.

### 14. Check for the abstract-of frame

"The weight of", "the shape of", "the sense of" — each is fine. Above 8 per
10,000 words, the construction becomes a substitute for concrete description.

## Part V — Knowing When to Stop

### 15. Diminishing returns feel like clarity

Every successful revision cycle creates the urge to add "just one more
mechanism" — one more pass, one more metric, one more review. Resist.
When your improvement tracker can't name a specific passage that got better
in the last cycle, you're done.

### 16. The stranger version might be the book

If everyone (including you) identifies the same flaw, consider whether the
flaw is actually the book. Sometimes the inefficient, uncomfortable,
unresolved element is what makes the work yours instead of competent.

### 17. Leave some threads loose

If every detail connects to something else, the book reads as constructed.
Human-written stories have waste, asymmetry, local oddness. Protect at least
three details that pay off nowhere — they make the world feel older than
your outline.

## Part VI — The Non-Obvious

### 18. Thinking about alternate stories ≠ inhabiting them

An abstract architectural audit defended the incumbent against a strawman.
An actual rewrite discovered a third option neither side of the debate had
imagined. If you're deciding whether a different approach would work:
write 1,500 words of it. Don't argue about it.

### 19. Rereading tests honesty

If nothing changes meaning when you read the book a second time — if no
early detail suddenly carries weight it didn't carry before — then the
surface is doing all the work. The best books get bigger on reread because
details planted early acquire significance they didn't announce.

### 20. Your framework will develop taste preferences you didn't design

If you use a systematic revision process, watch for the moment it starts
rewarding specific categories of change ("character autonomy restored,"
"world texture deepened") because those are the categories its successful
changes fell into. Retrospective insight becoming prospective doctrine is
the subtlest form of self-deception.

---

## Summary principle

> The framework constrains weaknesses so that strengths can emerge.
> It cannot supply the strengths themselves.
